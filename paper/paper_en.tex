\title{Regime-Aware Multi-Country Tourism Demand Forecasting: Integrating Economic Signals and Cross-Country Dependencies}

\author{Nguyen-Tuan~Tran,
        Hoai-Bao~Phan,
        Thi-Thu-Nga~Le}

\IEEEtitleabstractindextext{%
\begin{abstract}
	Tourism demand forecasting plays an important role in destination planning and tourism-sector decision-making, particularly in an environment characterized by seasonal fluctuations, changing economic conditions, and unexpected disruptions. However, forecasting tourism demand across multiple countries remains challenging because destinations exhibit heterogeneous demand patterns and interconnected temporal dynamics. To address these challenges, this study develops RA-HE-DLinear, a regime-aware forecasting framework that incorporates economic and calendar information alongside temporal and cross-country signals to jointly forecast tourist arrivals and overnight stays.
	
	The framework is evaluated using monthly Eurostat data covering 152 destination--accommodation series and annual World Bank data spanning 203 economies. On 4,522 observed Eurostat test pairs, RA-HE-DLinear achieves weighted absolute percentage errors (WAPE) of 9.16\% for tourist arrivals and 9.64\% for overnight stays, improving upon Vanilla DLinear (9.36\% and 10.47\%, respectively). Nevertheless, Seasonal Naive remains more accurate, with WAPE values of 6.04\% and 5.68\%. During the pandemic evaluation period, the static forecast blend reduces arrivals WAPE from 76.98\% to 66.69\% relative to DLinear. On the annual World Bank dataset, the framework achieves WAPE values of 9.45\% for arrivals and 28.27\% for overnight stays, the latter based partly on imputed targets. These findings highlight the importance of accounting for differences in demand patterns and external economic conditions when forecasting tourism activity across destinations. The framework provides a complementary forecasting approach that may support tourism demand monitoring and destination-level planning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Tourism demand forecasting plays a foundational role in destination management, accommodation operations, and transport planning \cite{song2019tourism,clements1998forecasting}. In tourism economics, tourist arrivals ($Y^{\text{arr}}$) and overnight stays ($Y^{\text{nig}}$) represent two co-evolving primary indicators of market activity. Arrivals measure inbound visitor traffic across borders and points of entry, whereas overnight stays quantify the cumulative duration and intensity of accommodation utilization. Their ratio defines the average Length of Stay ($\text{LOS} = Y^{\text{nig}} / Y^{\text{arr}}$), a critical metric governing hotel occupancy, visitor expenditure, and destination capacity.

Despite extensive advances in predictive modeling, multi-country tourism demand forecasting faces four central methodological challenges: (1) \textit{Destination heterogeneity and structural breaks across phases}: destination panels exhibit divergent seasonal amplitudes and macroeconomic fundamentals, while external shocks—such as pandemics \cite{gossling2020pandemics}, energy crises, and inflation—induce abrupt regime shifts between expansion, crisis contraction, and recovery; (2) \textit{Transnational spillovers and multi-destination itineraries}: cross-border tourism flows display strong co-movements and corridor effects \cite{yang2019spatial,cang2014comparative}, yet spatial models often rely on rigid geographical distance matrices that fail to reflect aviation and economic connectivity, while channel-independent deep learning ignores cross-country links entirely; (3) \textit{Cross-indicator inconsistency}: standard univariate pipelines model arrivals and overnight stays independently, routinely producing physically impossible forecasts where visitors stay fewer nights than visitors arriving ($\hat{Y}^{\text{nig}} < \hat{Y}^{\text{arr}}$), directly violating the UNWTO definitional lower bound of $\text{LOS} \ge 1.0$ \cite{unwto2010recommendations}; and (4) \textit{Uncertainty quantification under temporal shifts}: tourism decision-makers operate under asymmetric downside risk, where traditional Gaussian prediction intervals fail under crisis-induced heavy tails and heteroskedasticity, while conformal prediction guarantees are challenged by time-series autocorrelation and distribution shifts \cite{barber2023conformal,gibbs2021adaptive}.

To address time-series forecasting, Long-term Time Series Forecasting (LTSF) linear decomposition models, notably DLinear \cite{zeng2023dlinear}, have emerged as an effective paradigm that challenges complex Transformers. By decomposing input sequences into moving-average trend and seasonal components before applying direct linear mappings, DLinear preserves temporal order and avoids self-attention overfitting on seasonal continuous series with substantially fewer parameters and computational FLOPs. However, vanilla DLinear operates as a strictly static, univariate, and channel-independent architecture. It cannot adapt to macroeconomic regime shifts, ignores transnational spatial spillovers, and does not enforce physical consistency constraints across coupled indicators.

To overcome these limitations, this study develops \textbf{RA-HE-DLinear} (\textit{Regime-Aware Hierarchical-Economic DLinear}), a lightweight framework that integrates economic signals and cross-country dependencies for the joint prediction of tourist arrivals and overnight stays. The framework grounds its architectural modules directly in tourism economics: (i) an \textit{HMM-conditioned temporal engine (RA-Exo-DLinear)} that infers latent market regimes (expansion, crisis, recovery) from aggregate arrivals growth, public health stringency \cite{hale2021oxford}, and accommodation price inflation \cite{eurostat_hicp_release}, dynamically modulating the baseline trend via soft-gated Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018film}; (ii) a \textit{cross-country factor model} using Temporal Regularized Matrix Factorization (TRMF) \cite{yu2016trmf} that extracts latent transnational co-movements without requiring pre-specified distance proxies; (iii) an \textit{out-of-sample validation gating mechanism} that dynamically balances destination-specific temporal forecasts against regional pooled factors; and (iv) an \textit{arrivals-anchored projection operator ($\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$)} that enforces $\text{LOS} \ge 1.0$ by treating reliable customs-verified arrivals as an invariant anchor, coupled with residual-conformal calibration \cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2021conformal} to deliver empirical downside-risk intervals under non-Gaussian errors.

The primary contributions of this work are fourfold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Regime-Aware Lightweight Core:} We couple LTSF linear decomposition with macroeconomic HMM state conditioning and soft-gated FiLM, maintaining low computational complexity while adapting to structural breaks.
    \item \textbf{Data-Driven Cross-Country Spillovers:} We formulate a transnational factor learning and adaptive fusion scheme via TRMF, capturing cross-destination co-movements without ad-hoc geographical contiguity assumptions.
    \item \textbf{Physical Consistency and Distribution-Free Calibration:} We introduce an arrivals-anchored projection operator $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$ that eliminates 100\% of operational $\text{LOS} < 1.0$ violations, combined with empirical conformal prediction intervals evaluated under non-exchangeable time-series conditions.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Multi-Country Benchmark:} We evaluate the framework across a monthly Eurostat panel (38 countries, 152 lodging series, 2010--2026) and an annual World Bank panel (203 economies, 1995--2020), including pandemic stress testing, factor ablation, and publication-lag sensitivity analysis.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related_work} reviews related work and synthesizes research gaps. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the RA-HE-DLinear methodology and its theoretical foundations. Section~\ref{sec:results} details experimental benchmarks, ablation studies, and sensitivity analyses. Section~\ref{sec:management} discusses managerial implications, Section~\ref{sec:limitations} addresses study limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Tourism demand forecasting has evolved across four distinct methodological paradigms: classical statistical models, machine learning ensembles, deep time-series neural networks, and domain-constrained coherent forecasting frameworks. This section synthesizes this progression, examines regime-switching and spatial spillover modeling in tourism, and delineates the specific research gaps addressed by this study.

\subsection{Evolution of Tourism Demand Forecasting}

Tourism demand forecasting has traditionally relied on statistical and econometric models. ARIMA, seasonal SARIMA, and Error-Trend-Seasonal (ETS) models established early benchmarks by capturing seasonal patterns in visitor counts \cite{athanasopoulos2011tourism}. Econometric approaches further incorporated explanatory variables such as GDP, relative consumer prices, and transportation costs \cite{song2019tourism,clements1998forecasting}. Although these models offer interpretability, their ability to capture nonlinear relationships and complex interactions across multiple destinations remains limited.

Machine learning methods later introduced greater flexibility in modeling tourism demand. Models such as Support Vector Regression (SVR), LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, and CatBoost \cite{dorogush2018catboost} enabled the use of calendar variables and macroeconomic indicators. Forecast combinations also became an effective approach to reducing dependence on individual model specifications \cite{claveria2016tourism}. However, conventional machine learning methods often require manual lag construction and may struggle to capture long-term temporal dependencies.

Deep learning approaches have further expanded tourism forecasting capabilities. Recurrent neural networks, particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have been used to model sequential patterns in tourism demand \cite{hochreiter1997lstm,law2019tourism}. More recently, Transformer-based architectures, including Informer \cite{zhou2021informer}, Autoformer \cite{wu2021autoformer}, PatchTST \cite{nie2023patchtst}, and iTransformer \cite{liu2024itransformer}, have addressed long-range temporal dependencies. Other architectures, such as TimesNet \cite{wu2023timesnet}, explore periodic patterns through multidimensional representations of time series.

Despite these developments, Zeng et al. \cite{zeng2023dlinear} showed that simple linear models, particularly DLinear, can outperform more complex Transformer architectures on several long-term forecasting benchmarks. DLinear decomposes a time series into trend and seasonal components before applying linear projections to generate forecasts. Its simplicity and computational efficiency make it a relevant baseline for long-term forecasting. However, DLinear does not explicitly model regime changes or cross-series relationships, nor does it enforce consistency between related forecasting variables.

\subsection{Regime Shifts and Markov-Switching in Tourism}

International tourism demand is sensitive to economic fluctuations and external shocks, which can lead to changes in travel patterns and demand dynamics \cite{gossling2020pandemics}. Hamilton \cite{hamilton1989regime} introduced Markov-switching autoregressive models to identify latent economic states and transitions between different phases of the business cycle.

Several studies have applied regime-switching approaches to tourism demand analysis. Claveria and Datzira \cite{claveria2010forecasting} compared Markov-switching models with conventional time-series approaches for forecasting tourism demand across four source markets to Catalonia. Valadkhani and O'Mahony \cite{valadkhani2018identifying} examined structural changes and regime switching across 53 international source markets for Australian tourism. Yao and Cao \cite{yao2020neural} further combined a hidden Markov model with an autoregressive neural network, using the HMM to capture seasonal patterns and the neural network to model the trend component. More recently, Botha and Saayman \cite{botha2022forecasting} employed Markov-switching models to forecast tourism demand across five destinations, incorporating changes in price and income elasticities across different regimes.

These studies demonstrate the application of regime-switching methods in tourism forecasting, including econometric and neural network-based approaches. Although the LTSF-Linear framework \cite{zeng2023dlinear} demonstrated the effectiveness of lightweight decomposition for long-term forecasting, it does not explicitly model spatial correlations between variates or incorporate latent economic regimes as conditioning information. Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018film} provides a general mechanism for conditioning neural representations on external information through affine transformations. However, the integration of probabilistic economic regime information into linear forecasting architectures, particularly for joint multi-destination tourism demand forecasting, remains insufficiently investigated.

\subsection{Cross-Country Dependencies and Latent Factors}

Tourism demand across destinations is often spatially interdependent. Previous studies have identified spillover effects in tourism flows, reflecting interactions between neighboring destinations and multi-destination travel behavior \cite{yang2012spatial}. Yang and Zhang \cite{yang2019spatiotemporal} further demonstrated the value of spatial-temporal models for tourism demand forecasting across 29 Chinese provincial regions, examining different spatial weighting matrices, including geographic and flow-based specifications. Jiao et al. \cite{jiao2020local} extended this direction by incorporating spatial dependence and spatial heterogeneity into tourism forecasting across 37 European countries. These studies highlight the importance of accounting for relationships between destinations rather than modeling each tourism market in isolation.

Spatial econometric models, including Spatial Autoregressive (SAR) and Spatial Durbin models, provide established frameworks for representing spatial interactions through predefined weighting matrices. Although these matrices can incorporate geographic, economic, or flow-based relationships, their specification requires prior assumptions about how destinations are connected. In contrast, channel-independent forecasting architectures such as PatchTST \cite{nie2023patchtst} process each time-series channel independently using shared model parameters. While this design reduces computational complexity, it does not explicitly incorporate historical information from other channels when generating individual forecasts. Thus, both approaches offer different ways of handling cross-series dependencies, but leave room for data-driven representations that can capture shared temporal patterns across destinations.

Temporal Regularized Matrix Factorization (TRMF), introduced by Yu et al. \cite{yu2016trmf}, provides a framework for learning low-dimensional latent representations from high-dimensional time-series data. By combining matrix factorization with temporal regularization, TRMF captures shared patterns across multiple series while modeling the temporal dynamics of latent factors. This makes it a relevant approach for representing dependencies in multi-destination tourism data. However, how such latent representations can be selectively incorporated into lightweight forecasting architectures remains an important direction for further investigation.

\subsection{Forecast Coherence and Operational Consistency}

Hierarchical reconciliation has been studied in tourism forecasting. Kourentzes and Athanasopoulos \cite{kourentzes2019coherent} developed a cross-temporal reconciliation approach for Australian tourism, ensuring that forecasts remain additive across geographical and temporal hierarchies. This framework represents hierarchical relationships through a summation structure, commonly expressed as $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}$.

However, hierarchical coherence does not address consistency between different tourism indicators. Tourist arrivals and overnight stays measure distinct aspects of tourism activity. According to the International Recommendations for Tourism Statistics \cite{unwto2010recommendations}, a visitor is classified as a tourist if the trip includes at least one overnight stay. In accommodation statistics, arrivals are recorded in the month of check-in, whereas overnight stays are allocated to the months in which the nights occur \cite{eurostat_tourism_release}. Consequently, the monthly ratio of overnight stays to arrivals cannot be interpreted directly as the average length of stay of the visitors arriving in that month, particularly when stays extend across reporting periods.

Despite this temporal difference, the relationship between arrivals and overnight stays may provide a useful operational constraint when supported by the observed data. In this study, we examine whether aggregate monthly overnight stays consistently meet or exceed arrivals within the forecasting scope. If this relationship is supported by the data, we impose a minimum ratio of $\text{LOS}_{\min}=1.0$ through an arrivals-anchored projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$. This projection adjusts forecasts that violate the specified lower bound while preserving the arrivals forecast as the reference. Unlike hierarchical reconciliation, which enforces additive relationships across aggregation levels, this constraint addresses a domain-specific relationship between two tourism indicators.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification and Conformal Prediction}

Reliable uncertainty estimates are important for tourism planning, particularly during periods of substantial demand fluctuations and external shocks \cite{gossling2020pandemics}. Parametric prediction intervals, such as those derived from Gaussian-based forecasting models, depend on assumptions about the predictive error distribution. When the error distribution is skewed, heavy-tailed, or changes over time, these intervals may provide inadequate coverage.

Conformal prediction offers a distribution-free framework for constructing prediction intervals. Introduced by Vovk et al. \cite{vovk2005algorithmic} and reviewed by Angelopoulos and Bates \cite{angelopoulos2021conformal}, split conformal prediction uses a separate calibration set to estimate prediction errors and construct intervals with finite-sample marginal coverage of at least $1-\alpha$, provided that the calibration and test observations are exchangeable. This guarantee does not require a specified parametric error distribution. However, temporal dependence and distribution shifts can violate exchangeability, limiting the validity of standard split-conformal intervals in non-stationary time series \cite{barber2023conformal}.

Several extensions address this limitation. Gibbs and Candès \cite{gibbs2021adaptive} introduced adaptive conformal inference, which adjusts prediction intervals online to target long-run coverage under distribution shifts. Barber et al. \cite{barber2023conformal} further developed conformal methods beyond exchangeability and analyzed the loss of coverage under dependence and distribution drift. These developments highlight the distinction between standard finite-sample coverage under exchangeability and coverage objectives under changing data distributions.

Probabilistic tourism forecasting has also received attention. For example, Cui et al. \cite{cui2026patchtstgmm} combined PatchTST with a Gaussian mixture model to construct prediction intervals for tourism demand. Nevertheless, evaluating interval calibration across temporally dependent and post-shock forecasting periods remains relevant, particularly when the objective is to assess empirical coverage rather than claim an unconditional theoretical guarantee. In this study, split-conformal calibration is applied to rolling forecast residuals as an empirical uncertainty assessment. Its performance is evaluated across forecasting folds using coverage and interval-width metrics, without assuming that exchangeability holds across the entire time series.

Taken together, the reviewed studies address temporal forecasting, regime changes, cross-destination dependencies, indicator consistency, and predictive uncertainty from different methodological perspectives. This study brings these aspects into a unified forecasting framework. RA-HE-DLinear combines regime-conditioned temporal modeling, latent cross-destination factors, an operational consistency projection between arrivals and overnight stays, and conformal interval calibration. The resulting framework is evaluated not only through point-forecast accuracy but also through operational consistency and empirical interval coverage under changing demand conditions.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
For entity $i$, let $\mathbf{y}_{i,t}=[Y^{\text{arr}}_{i,t},Y^{\text{nig}}_{i,t}]^T$ denote arrivals and nights. We predict $H$ future periods from $L$ past target observations, past macroeconomic indicators, known future calendar features, and the origin-specific regime belief. Paired predictions satisfy
\begin{equation}
\hat{Y}_{i, \tau}^{\text{nig}} \ge \hat{Y}_{i, \tau}^{\text{arr}} \times \text{LOS}_{\min}, \quad \forall \tau \in \{t+1, \dots, t+H\},
\label{eq:physical_floor}
\end{equation}
where $\text{LOS}_{\min}=1.0$ night is the imposed accommodation consistency floor \cite{unwto2010recommendations}.

\subsection{Regime-Aware Temporal Engine (RA-Exo-DLinear)}
\label{sec:regime}
\noindent \textbf{HMM conditioning.} A three-state Gaussian HMM uses aggregate year-over-year arrivals growth, COVID stringency \cite{hale2021oxford}, and accommodation prices \cite{eurostat_hicp_release}. Signal scalers and HMM parameters are fitted on training data and frozen. For inputs admitted at origin $o$, forward-filtered beliefs are
\begin{equation}
\alpha_t^{(o)}(k) \propto p\big(\mathbf{u}_t^{(o)} \mid S_t=k\big) \sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\alpha_{t-1}^{(o)}(j)A_{j,k},
\label{eq:hmm_forward}
\end{equation}
normalized to sum to one. IDs 1, 0, and 2 rank low, middle, and high fitted training-growth centroids within each fold. The most probable origin state selects an eight-dimensional embedding
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_r = \mathbf{E}_{\hat{s}_o, :} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_r}, \qquad
\hat{s}_o = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{k \in \{0,\dots,K-1\}} \alpha_o^{(o)}(k).
\label{eq:regime_embedding}
\end{equation}

\noindent \textbf{Decomposition and initialization.} Each target channel is split by a centered moving average with endpoint replication and monthly kernel $K_{\text{ma}}=13$ \cite{zeng2023dlinear}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{\text{trend}} = \operatorname{AvgPool}(\mathbf{X}, K_{\text{ma}}), \quad \mathbf{X}_{\text{seasonal}} = \mathbf{X} - \mathbf{X}_{\text{trend}}.
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
The $H\times L$ projections $\mathbf{W}_T$ and $\mathbf{W}_S$ initialize to last-step persistence and seasonal-lag replication, respectively. They remain frozen in the benchmark while the enabled modulation heads are trained.

\noindent \textbf{FiLM and exogenous inputs.} With projected trend $\mathbf{z}_{T,k}=\mathbf{W}_T\mathbf{X}_{\text{trend},k}$, target-specific FiLM \cite{perez2018film} uses gate $g_{r,k}=\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{g,k}^T\mathbf{e}_r+b_{g,k})$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
f_{\text{trend}, k} &= \big(1 + g_{r,k} \tilde{\gamma}_k(\mathbf{e}_r)\big) \mathbf{z}_{T,k} + g_{r,k} \beta_k(\mathbf{e}_r)\mathbf{1}_H, \\
\tilde{\gamma}_k(\mathbf{e}_r) &= \alpha_{\text{trend}, k} \tanh(\mathbf{w}_{\gamma, k}^T \mathbf{e}_r + b_{\gamma, k}), \\
\beta_k(\mathbf{e}_r) &= \mathbf{w}_{\beta, k}^T \mathbf{e}_r + b_{\beta, k}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:film_trend}
\end{equation}
Gate weights initialize to zero and $b_{g,k}=-3.0$, giving $g_{r,k}\approx0.047$ initially.

Standardized future-calendar and past-macroeconomic inputs generate bounded target-specific adjustments:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\delta_{\text{fut}, k, h} &= \alpha_{\text{fut}, k} \tanh(\mathbf{w}_{F, k}^T \mathbf{x}^{\text{fut}}_h + b_{F, k}), \; h = 1, \dots, H, \\
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{past}, k} &= \alpha_{\text{past}, k} \tanh\big(\mathbf{W}_{P, k} \operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{past}}) + \mathbf{b}_{P, k}\big),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:exo_streams}
\end{equation}
The future projection acts at each horizon, while $\mathbf{W}_{P,k}$ maps the flattened historical window to $H$ steps. Trainable scales $(\alpha_{\text{trend},k},\alpha_{\text{fut},k},\alpha_{\text{past},k})$ initialize to $(0.35,0.25,0.20)$, with modulation weights initialized to zero.

\noindent \textbf{Baseline scaling and synthesis.} The training-only baseline $S_{\text{base},k}(i,h)$ is the entity's median count for the forecast month, floored at one. Annual panels use the entity training median. The additive forecast combines trend, seasonality, and baseline-scaled exogenous adjustments \cite{hastie1990gam}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{temporal}, k} = \operatorname{ReLU}\Big(& \underbrace{f_{\text{trend}, k}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{trend}, k}, \mathbf{e}_r)}_{\text{Regime-modulated trend}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{W}_S \mathbf{X}_{\text{seasonal}, k}}_{\text{Seasonal cycle}} \\
&+ \underbrace{\mathbf{S}_{\text{base}, k} \odot \boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{fut}, k}}_{\text{Future calendar effect}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{S}_{\text{base}, k} \odot \boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{past}, k}}_{\text{Past macro shocks}}\Big).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:temporal_forecast}
\end{equation}
Disabling FiLM and exogenous projections recovers the nonnegative DLinear control.

\subsection{Cross-Country Factor Modeling and Adaptive Fusion}
\label{sec:trmf}
TRMF uses aggregate accommodation training matrices for arrivals and nights. Eurostat fold dimensions are $38\times72$, $38\times96$, $38\times120$, and $38\times144$, with $203\times21$ for World Bank. After filling training gaps with training-only fallbacks, rows are standardized by their means and standard deviations:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{Y}_{k,i,t} = \frac{Y_{k,i,t}-\mu_{k,i}}{\sigma_{k,i}+10^{-5}}, \qquad \widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}_k \approx \mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{X}_k^T,
\label{eq:trmf_scaling}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{W}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times R}$ and $\mathbf{X}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{T_b\times R}$ are spatial loadings and temporal factors, with rank $R=4$ \cite{yu2016trmf}:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{X}_k, \boldsymbol{\Theta}_k} \|\widetilde{\mathbf{Y}}_k - \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{X}_k^T\|_F^2 + \lambda_w \|\mathbf{W}_k\|_F^2 + \lambda_x \mathcal{R}_{\text{AR}}(\mathbf{X}_k, \boldsymbol{\Theta}_k),
\label{eq:trmf_objective}
\end{equation}
The AR lag set is $\{1,2,12\}$ monthly and $\{1,2\}$ annually. Factors are extrapolated recursively as $\mathbf{x}_{k,t+h}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k,l}\odot\mathbf{x}_{k,t+h-l}$ and rescaled by the training statistics. Country forecasts are allocated to category $c$ using training shares:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_{k,i,c} &= \frac{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_b}y_{k,i,c,t}}{\max\!\left(1,\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_b}y_{k,i,\mathrm{total},t}\right)}, \\
\hat{y}^{\mathrm{spatial}}_{k,i,c,t+h} &= s_{k,i,c}\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{country}}_{k,i,t+h}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:trmf_category_mapping}
\end{equation}

\noindent \textbf{Spatial--temporal fusion.} Temporal and spatial forecasts are blended \cite{jacobs1991adaptive,shazeer2017moe}:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{fused}} = w_{\text{time}} \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{temporal}} + (1 - w_{\text{time}}) \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{spatial}}.
\label{eq:convex_blend}
\end{equation}
The static blend selects a sigmoid weight on held-out validation data using regularized Brent optimization:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\theta^* &= \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}} \left\{ \mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}\big(\sigma(\theta)\big) + \lambda \theta^2 \right\}, \\
w_{\text{time}}^* &= \sigma(\theta^*) = (1 + e^{-\theta^*})^{-1},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:static_gate}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}$ averages arrivals and nights WAPE, and $\lambda=0.005$. The adaptive configuration instead searches $w\in[0,1]$ and accepts blending only when it improves on the temporal forecast:
\begin{equation}
w_{\text{time}}^{\text{adapt}} = \begin{cases}
\hat{w}, & \text{if } \mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(\hat{w}) < \mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(1.0) - \epsilon, \\
1.0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:adaptive_fallback}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon=10^{-5}$. Otherwise, the temporal forecast receives full weight.

\subsection{Economic Constraint Projection \texorpdfstring{($\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$)}{(Pi\_C)}}
For $\mathcal{C}=\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}_+^2:v\ge u\}$, training combines normalized count loss with a penalty on lower-bound violations:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} &= \sum_{k \in \{\text{arr}, \text{nig}\}} \left\|\frac{\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_k - \mathbf{Y}_k}{\mathbf{S}_{\text{base}, k}}\right\|_2^2 \\
&\quad + \lambda_{\text{cons}} \left\|\frac{\operatorname{ReLU}(\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{arr}} - \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_{\text{nig}})}{\mathbf{S}_{\text{base}, \text{arr}}}\right\|_2^2,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:coupled_loss}
\end{equation}
Division is elementwise and $\lambda_{\text{cons}}=0.2$. The inference projection anchors arrivals and raises nights when needed:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\hat{Y}_{\text{arr}}, \hat{Y}_{\text{nig}}) = \left(\hat{Y}_{\text{arr}}, \, \max\left(\hat{Y}_{\text{nig}}, \, \hat{Y}_{\text{arr}} \times 1.0\right)\right).
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
This operator preserves nonnegative arrivals and enforces the lower bound on nights.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification (Conformal DCQF)}
\label{sec:conformal_method}
Split conformal calibration uses held-out observed pairs after projection \cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2021conformal}, with target-specific absolute residuals
\begin{equation}
s_{i, k} = \big|y_{i, k} - \hat{Y}_{i, k}^*\big|, \qquad k \in \{\text{arr}, \text{nig}\}.
\end{equation}
For $\alpha=0.10$, let $\hat{Q}_{1-\alpha,k}$ be the empirical quantile at level $\lceil(N_{\text{cal}}+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/N_{\text{cal}}$. The interval is
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{1-\alpha, k} = \left[\max\left(0, \hat{Y}_k^* - \hat{Q}_{1-\alpha, k}\right), \, \hat{Y}_k^* + \hat{Q}_{1-\alpha, k}\right].
\label{eq:conformal_interval}
\end{equation}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} summarizes fitting and inference.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{RA-HE-DLinear Training and Inference}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Chronological training, validation, calibration, and test partitions.
\STATE Fit signal scalers, HMM parameters, TRMF factors, category shares, and baseline medians on training data.
\STATE Initialize and freeze trend/seasonal projections. Train enabled modulation heads using Eq.~\eqref{eq:coupled_loss}.
\STATE Select the fusion weight on validation using Eq.~\eqref{eq:static_gate} or Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive_fallback}.
\STATE Project calibration forecasts and estimate residual quantiles.
\FOR{each forecast origin $o$}
    \STATE Filter admissible HMM inputs and form temporal and spatial forecasts.
    \STATE Fuse, project via Eq.~\eqref{eq:projection}, and construct intervals via Eq.~\eqref{eq:conformal_interval}.
\ENDFOR
\RETURN Point forecasts and prediction intervals.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Empirical evaluation reveals that \textbf{Seasonal Naive} achieves the lowest overall count error across both variables, with WAPE values of \ReviewerSNaiveArrivalWAPE{}\% for tourist arrivals and \ReviewerSNaiveNightsWAPE{}\% for overnight stays (Table~\ref{tab:reviewer_benchmark}). This result underscores the strong seasonal persistence inherent in European leisure tourism: during post-crisis recovery and tranquil operational regimes, replicating prior-year seasonal counts ($y_{t-12}$) provides a robust baseline with zero parameter estimation variance, outperforming complex machine learning estimators that carry parametric risk. Consequently, we do not claim that the proposed deep learning framework achieves the lowest overall count error across all possible models.

Instead, the core scientific contributions and empirical value of \textbf{RA-HE-DLinear (Adaptive Gated + $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$)} reside in three key properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy Among Learned Models:} Among learned time-series architectures, RA-HE-DLinear achieves the highest accuracy, recording \ReviewerArrivalWAPE{}\% arrivals WAPE (MAE \ReviewerArrivalMAE{}) and \ReviewerNightsWAPE{}\% nights WAPE (MAE \ReviewerNightsMAE{}). It achieves lower prediction errors than Vanilla DLinear (9.36\% arrivals, 10.47\% nights) and classical econometric baselines (ETS at 12.69\%/13.49\%, SARIMA at 28.19\%/26.37\%, and LightGBM at 42.51\%/45.91\%). Error reduction over Vanilla DLinear is statistically significant on overnight stays under a paired $t$-test ($t = 3.14$, $p = 0.0017$) and non-parametric Wilcoxon tests ($p < 10^{-5}$ for arrivals, $p < 10^{-14}$ for nights).
    \item \textbf{Strict Operational and Physical Consistency:} In tourism economics, by definition an overnight tourist stays at least one night ($\text{LOS} \ge 1.0$), imposing the fundamental operational lower bound $\hat{Y}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{nig}} \ge \hat{Y}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{arr}}$. Standard unconstrained models routinely violate this bound, yielding negative implied durations of stay. We define the \textbf{Physical Violation Rate} ($\operatorname{PVR}$) as the percentage of test forecast pairs violating this constraint:
    \begin{equation}
    \operatorname{PVR} = \frac{100\%}{N_{\mathrm{pairs}}} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\mathrm{pairs}}} \mathbb{I}\left(\hat{Y}_k^{\mathrm{nig}} < \hat{Y}_k^{\mathrm{arr}}\right),
    \label{eq:physical_violation_rate}
    \end{equation}
    evaluated over all $N_{\mathrm{pairs}} = 4,712$ forecast instances. While unconstrained models exhibit operational inconsistencies---reaching 8.786\% for SARIMAX, 2.504\% for ETS, 2.419\% for LightGBM, 1.931\% for DLinear, and even 0.085\% for Seasonal Naive---RA-HE-DLinear completely eliminates physical violations (0.000\%), guaranteeing operational plausibility for municipal budgeting and lodging inventory planning.
    \item \textbf{Protection Against Spatial Distribution Shift:} When spatial matrix factorization is statically blended without gating (\textbf{RA-HE Static}), performance degrades to 11.62\% arrivals and 13.56\% nights WAPE---inferior to Vanilla DLinear. This degradation stems from post-pandemic structural realignment: rigid historical spatial correlation matrices induce error spillover from neighboring destinations undergoing divergent recovery paths. The \textbf{adaptive validation gate} resolves this vulnerability by dynamically evaluating blending gain against pure temporal forecasts on held-out validation data ($w_{\mathrm{time}}^* \to 1.0$), shielding the model from adverse spatial shift during calm regimes while preserving multi-country stabilization during crises.
\end{enumerate}

The anomalous failure of \textbf{SARIMAX} on tourist arrivals (WAPE 160.60\%, MAE 2,226,629) warrants specific examination. Fitted on 2010--2021 with unscaled macroeconomic inputs (HICP inflation indices and stringency), 233 out of 304 SARIMAX fits failed numerical convergence entirely, reverting to the pre-declared seasonal fallback. Among the 71 converged series, the sharp post-COVID European inflation surge (HICP rising 30--40\% above historical levels) interacted with unregularized positive coefficients under the inverse logarithmic transform $\exp(\cdot)-1$. In 23 destination-category series, this caused exponential prediction divergence (e.g., Belgian hotel arrivals forecasted above 4.8 million vs.\ actual 1.1 million), inflating aggregate WAPE ($100\sum|\hat{y}-y|/\sum y$). For overnight stays, where unregularized coefficients did not trigger runaway exponentials, SARIMAX recorded 17.70\% WAPE. This stark disparity illustrates the severe fragility of classical unregularized log-linear estimators under out-of-distribution macroeconomic shocks, contrasting sharply with the stable bounded behavior of RA-HE-DLinear. Furthermore, point-in-time publication-lag sensitivity evaluations confirm that under simulated two-month accommodation and one-month HICP reporting delays, RA-HE-DLinear maintains robust stability, with WAPE increasing by only 0.28 percentage points for arrivals and 0.25 points for nights.

\subsection{Length-of-Stay (LOS) and Operational Consistency}
\label{sec:los_analysis}
The ratio of overnight stays to arrivals defines the destination's aggregate Length of Stay ($\operatorname{LOS} = Y^{\text{nig}} / Y^{\text{arr}}$). From an operational standpoint, predicting LOS is essential for hotel room inventory, housekeeping scheduling, and municipal tax revenue projections. For count errors $\epsilon_A = \hat{Y}^{\text{arr}} - Y^{\text{arr}}$ and $\epsilon_N = \hat{Y}^{\text{nig}} - Y^{\text{nig}}$, the derived LOS prediction error decomposes into:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\operatorname{LOS}}-\operatorname{LOS}
=\frac{\epsilon_N-\operatorname{LOS}\,\epsilon_A}{\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{arr}}}.
\label{eq:los_error_decomposition}
\end{equation}
As shown in Eq.~\eqref{eq:los_error_decomposition}, proportional errors between arrivals and nights can cancel, but small predicted arrivals denominators $\hat{Y}^{\text{arr}} \approx 0$ severely amplify ratio errors. 

\input{tables/reviewer_los.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:reviewer_los} summarizes LOS MAE and RMSE across evaluation folds on common defined denominators. During the pandemic crisis, unconstrained DLinear produced near-zero arrivals denominators (e.g., in Iceland during March 2021), causing its LOS RMSE to explode to 9.394 nights per arrival and generating 82 undefined ratios. In contrast, the spatial-temporal blend stabilized arrivals denominators, reducing pandemic LOS RMSE to 1.253 and eliminating undefined predictions entirely.

In the recent test period (1,122 common destination-months), adaptive RA-HE-DLinear achieves an LOS MAE of 0.167 nights per arrival and RMSE of 0.342 after applying the consistency projection $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$, outperforming Vanilla DLinear (MAE 0.182, RMSE 0.435). While individual visitor length of stay requires micro-level survey data, enforcing $\text{LOS}_{\min} = 1.0$ at the macro aggregate level serves as a robust operational guardrail that prevents physically contradictory forecasts. Across the recent test panel, the projection operator $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$ resolves 106 physical violations, reducing nights WAPE from 9.67\% to \ReviewerNightsWAPE{}\% with arrivals held invariant as an anchor, while split-conformal calibration on observed 2023 pairs achieves nominal 90\% coverage (\ReviewerArrivalCoverage{}\% for arrivals and \ReviewerNightsCoverage{}\% for overnight stays).

\subsection{Component Contributions and Ablation Analysis}
\label{sec:component_contributions}
To systematically isolate the individual predictive contributions, marginal sensitivities, and architectural interactions of the proposed RA-HE-DLinear framework, we evaluate two complementary ablation protocols: a single-seed progressive component ladder (Table~\ref{tab:reviewer_ablation}) and a multi-seed $2^3$ factorial interaction experiment (Table~\ref{tab:factorial_effects}). The factorial design explicitly toggles three core modular components: direct exogenous calendar and macroeconomic projections ($E$), hidden Markov model macroeconomic regime states ($H$), and multiplicative feature-wise linear modulation ($F$).

\input{tables/ablation_combined.tex}

As documented in Table~\ref{tab:reviewer_ablation}, each architectural innovation addresses a specific econometric failure mode of the linear time-series baseline:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Exogenous Feature Projections ($E$):} Incorporating calendar schedules (harmonics, holiday counts) and macroeconomic covariates (HICP inflation, COVID stringency) yields the largest single accuracy gain. On the single-seed ladder, adding exogenous projections reduces arrivals WAPE from 9.36\% to 9.16\% and nights WAPE from 10.47\% to 9.67\%. Across the three-seed factorial contrasts (Table~\ref{tab:factorial_effects}), enabling $E$ delivers an average conditional error reduction of $-1.75\pm0.74$ percentage points (pp) for fused arrivals and $-2.59\pm0.61$ pp for fused nights ($\Delta\text{MAE} = -24,308$ arrivals, $-100,546$ nights).
    \item \textbf{Macroeconomic Regime Conditioning ($H$):} Providing the temporal backbone with structural HMM regime state embeddings contributes a steady conditional error reduction of $-0.14\pm0.05$ pp for arrivals and $-0.12\pm0.18$ pp for nights. While numerical gains on tranquil post-recovery data are modest, regime states provide essential macroeconomic context that anchors temporal representations.
    \item \textbf{Multiplicative Modulation ($F$) and Soft Gating:} When multiplicative FiLM modulation is applied without gating ($g_r = 1$), it severely degrades performance, inflating fused WAPE by $+3.37\pm1.51$ pp for arrivals and $+5.69\pm2.13$ pp for nights (Table~\ref{tab:factorial_effects}). This deterioration occurs because unconstrained affine gain over-amplifies noisy transient fluctuations, corrupting the robust linear sequence backbone. This empirical finding provides direct econometric justification for our soft-gating mechanism ($g_{r,k} = \sigma(\mathbf{w}_g^T \mathbf{e}_r + b_g)$ with bias initialization $b_g = -3.0$), which suppresses unregularized gain scaling and preserves linear trend stability.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Spillover (TRMF) and Adaptive Fallback:} Cross-country spatial factor modeling provides effective stabilization during systemic crises (lowering pandemic arrivals WAPE from 76.98\% to 66.69\%, Table~\ref{tab:reviewer_regimes}). However, under post-crisis structural divergence, an unconstrained spatial blend incurs spatial distribution shift (Table~\ref{tab:reviewer_ablation}, row 6: 14.82\% arrivals, 16.66\% nights). The adaptive validation gate resolves this trade-off by dynamically setting $w_{\text{time}}^* \to 1.0$ during stable periods, securing optimal local accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Operational Constraint Projection ($\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$):} The asymmetric projection operator $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$ enforces the physical lower bound $\hat{Y}^{\mathrm{nig}} \ge \hat{Y}^{\mathrm{arr}}$, reducing adaptive nights MAE from 374,958 to 373,738 (WAPE from 9.67\% to 9.64\%) while eliminating 100\% of physical violations (from 2.250\% down to 0.000\%).
\end{enumerate}
Together, these ablation benchmarks demonstrate that the predictive performance of RA-HE-DLinear stems not from arbitrary model complexity, but from the deliberate, synergistic combination of linear parsimony, exogenous conditioning, soft-gated regime modulation, and physical consistency enforcement.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations qualify the operational generalizability of this study:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Capacity and Price Optimization:} The framework evaluates demand volume predictions rather than revenue management optimization. Integrating capacity caps, room rate elasticity, and staffing cost functions remains necessary for direct operational automation.
    \item \textbf{Reporting Lag and Release Vintages:} While point-in-time sensitivity evaluations confirm robustness to simulated two-month reporting lags, complete real-time release timestamp archives were unavailable for historical Eurostat vintages.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Overnight Stays in Global Data:} In the World Bank annual panel, non-OECD overnight stays rely on regional length-of-stay coefficients due to the absence of unified global accommodation registries, limiting definitive conclusions on global lodging nights.
    \item \textbf{Single-Period Regime Uniformity:} Within individual forecast origins, origin-specific HMM beliefs are evaluated as static regimes, meaning intra-horizon transitions between states during unexpected crises warrant further dynamic online updating mechanisms.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This study develops RA-HE-DLinear, an economic-regime-aware forecasting framework designed for the joint prediction of tourist arrivals and overnight stays across multi-country destination panels. By coupling LTSF linear sequence decomposition with HMM macroeconomic regime conditioning, TRMF data-driven spatial factorization, validation-guarded adaptive fusion, and an arrivals-anchored projection operator $\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}$, the framework addresses the structural trade-offs between computational parsimony, regime sensitivity, and physical consistency.

Empirical evaluation across 38 European destinations (152 lodging series, 2010--2026) and 203 global economies demonstrates key insights: (i) Seasonal Naive remains the strongest count baseline in stable periods, confirming heavy seasonal autocorrelation in leisure demand; (ii) RA-HE-DLinear achieves lower prediction errors than Vanilla DLinear and classical econometric models, recording \ReviewerArrivalWAPE{}\% arrivals and \ReviewerNightsWAPE{}\% nights WAPE while eliminating physical lower-bound violations (0.000\% PVR); (iii) cross-country spatial factor modeling provides effective stabilization during severe demand collapses (lowering pandemic arrivals WAPE from 76.98\% to 66.69\%); and (iv) the largest predictive improvements occur in higher-risk upper-middle-income destinations. Future research will explore real-time vintage data streaming, high-frequency flight booking indicators, and online adaptive conformal inference under non-stationary regime transitions.
